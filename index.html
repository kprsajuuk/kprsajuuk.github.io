About

I am a software engineer. For seven years I built the layer of enterprise security tooling that people actually touch — static analysis, software composition analysis, supply chain monitoring — which in practice meant translating what an analysis engine can do into something a developer, a security team or an engineering director can configure and act on. The hard problem in that domain is not detection. It is noise: a scanner that cries wolf gets switched off, and a switched-off scanner detects nothing at all.

That work pulled me steadily upstream. Making the frontend fast and low-coupling freed up capacity; the capacity turned into requirements work, then module design, then product ownership of two zero-to-one platforms — while I kept owning frontend delivery the whole time. What I learned along the way is that a product decision is usually an architecture decision wearing a different hat. You cannot design an interactive call graph for a multi-user web application without first deciding how graph data gets cached, persisted and modelled.

Music is the other place I do this. I compose in Ableton Live, and the tools I build for it — a real-time visualizer, a probabilistic beat generator, a piano-roll sketchpad, a Markov-chain melody generator that learns from the clips already in your session — are the same exercise pointed at a different user: take something technically dense, an FFT buffer or a variable-order Markov model, and put it behind controls a musician will reach for mid-idea. Being the demanding user of my own tools has done more for my judgement about interaction design than reading about it ever did.

I came back to school because I wanted to look at the abstractions underneath the code again, and because AI is changing what this job is. I am extending from the interface layer out to end-to-end software engineering, and I am looking for a Summer 2027 internship where that extension happens on a real team.
